### Magna Carta of 1215 
On June 15, 1215 King John of England signed a royal document called **The Magna Carta** (Great Charter). It was a emergency peace treaty between rebellious group of barons and the tyrannical King John. The Barons were wealthy, powerful noblemen who held large estates directly from the king in exchange for military service and taxes. The King signed The Magna Carta because the the nobles captured London (which was Political & financial capital of England at that time) and forced the king to negotiate a peace deal at Runnymede.

But why Barons were angry over the king? Because the King demanded too much money from the nobles to pay for failed wars and he also acted above the law, jailed people without a fair reason, and broke promises.

However, Magna Carta of 1215 did not make England a constitutional monarchy because it was merely a flawed emergency peace treaty rather than a blueprint for a democratic government.

### Understanding Parliament & Judiciary
Back in 1215, England was run by a central body called the Curia Regis which was basically the "King’s Court". Think of it exactly like an Indian Darbar. Only the King, his family, and his closest, wealthiest noble friends (the Barons and Bishops) used to sit and be a part of that elite circle. Inside, they feasted, drank, made laws, and judged criminals, while ordinary citizens were only allowed in if they were serving food or being put on trial. It served three massive roles at once: it was a political cabinet for planning administration, a royal advisory board, and a powerful court where the King personally handled major public criminal cases to maintain law and order.

> [!info] Who were Bishops?
> In 1215, a bishop was a high-ranking church leader who ruled over a large region of local churches, but they lived more like wealthy politicians than humble priests. They were essentially powerful CEOs in religious robes, holding immense spiritual authority while owning massive estates that rivaled the country's richest noble lords. While they did not literally own merchants, they owned the towns and markets where merchants traded, allowing them to collect steep taxes, rents, and a mandatory 10% income tax from everyone in their territory.

Before Magna Carta, the Curia Regis was a single, all-powerful royal council made up of major barons and bishops, and the King called them whenever he needed money or advice, but it was totally arbitrary. 

#### The 1178 Judicial Spit
Even before 1215, this massive council's workload was already too heavy to handle in one room, and it was starting to split into two distinct lanes: a **judicial lane** and a **political/legislative lane**. Back in 1178, King Henry II got tired of dragging a massive courtroom of judges, legal documents, and criminal trials behind him while traveling the country. To fix this, he selected a permanent group of judges to stay in one fixed location to handle everyday lawsuits. Over time, this judicial side completely branched off into specialized professional courts:
* **The Court of Common Pleas:** Handled property and everyday civil disputes between citizens (a setup later locked down by Magna Carta Clause 17, forcing these trials to stay in one fixed place instead of following the king).
* **The Court of King's Bench:** Handled serious criminal cases directly involving the King's peace.
* **The Exchequer:** Split off to strictly handle royal money, debts, and tax auditing.

Meanwhile, the rest of the council stayed on the **political and legislative lane** with the King to handle governance, laws, and national taxes which is the track that kept growing toward Parliament.


After the famous Magna Carta was signed in 1215, King John agreed that he could no longer levy scutage (shield money/taxes) or "aid" without the "common counsel of our kingdom." To get this approval, the king had to summon a broader group, not just his immediate rich buddies, but archbishops, bishops, earls, and major barons through written summons.

The room was expanded into the "Great Council." It now included all the top noblemen and church leaders in England. It was still a room full of billionaires and warlords. Ordinary townspeople had absolutely no say. 

In 1265, a rebel nobleman named **Simon de Montfort** overthrew the King temporarily. To get the country on his side, he did something radical. He held a massive meeting and invited ordinary knights and town merchants into the Curia Regis for the very first time. However, once the King took power back, this practice was mostly stopped.

#### Model Parliament - 1295
In 1295, King Edward I looked at the history and decided to make the rebel's idea permanent. He opened the doors of that "one massive room" and officially invited the common townspeople back inside alongside the wealthy lords. It became the "Model" because from that year onward, the King had to invite the commoners into the room whenever he wanted to pass major taxes. So 1295 was the exact moment ordinary citizens finally got a ticket to enter it.

#### The 1178 Judicial Split (2nd Version)
Even though 1295 sorted out who got to sit in the political room, the old "Curia Regis" had another major problem: it was trying to act as a parliament *and* a Supreme Court all at once. Dragging a massive room full of judges, legal documents, and criminal trials behind a traveling king was a total nightmare. 

To fix this, the 13th century saw *the judicial functions break away* from the main council into permanent, *specialized courts:*
* **The Court of Common Pleas:** Handled property and everyday disputes between citizens (helped by Magna Carta, which forced these trials to stay in one fixed location instead of dragging across the country).
* **The Court of King's Bench:** Handled serious criminal cases directly involving the King's peace.
* **The Exchequer:** Split off to strictly handle royal money and tax auditing.

By the time the 1300s rolled around, the old all-in-one *Curia Regis* was officially dead & its political side had evolved into Parliament (legislative), and its legal side had turned into professional courts (judiciary).

#### The Great Split (1341)
By the 1330s, the "one massive room" setup was bursting at the seams. King Edward III (grandson of Edward I) was on the throne, and he was fighting a massive, expensive conflict called the Hundred Years' War. Because he needed constant funding, Parliament met more frequently than ever before. This intense pressure caused the single room of Westminster Hall to fracture permanently in 1341.

```

    [ 1295: ONE MASSIVE ROOM (Westminster Hall)]
    (King + Lords + Clergy + Knights + Burgesses)
                   │
                   ▼ (1341 Split)
       ┌────────────────────────────────┐
       ▼                                ▼
   HOUSE OF LORDS            HOUSE OF COMMONS
(King, Nobles, Bishops)  (Knights,Town Burgesses)
Met in Westminster Hall  Met in Westminster Abbey
```


With this move, Parliament officially split into two distinct bodies:
- **The House of Lords**: The original massive room (Westminster Hall). It contained the people who were there by birthright: the wealthy Earls, Barons, and Bishops.
- **The House of Commons**: The new separate room (Westminster Abbey). It contained the people who were elected to represent their local communities (communes).

**Invention of a Speaker** (1377) :- Because the Commons were now in a different room, they needed a way to talk to the King without all 200 of them marching back into his hall. In 1377, they elected their very first Speaker. The Speaker's job was to listen to the private debates in the Commons' room, walk over to the King's room, and "speak" on behalf of the common people. This phase completely fixed the problem of the intimidating "single room" and created the two-house layout that both the UK and India still use today.

### Charles I - 1625
Why did England have a Civil War?

Between 1642 and 1651, England was torn apart by a bunch of wars between Parliament and Royalists that resulted in the deaths of roughly 190,000 people, including the King of England, Charles I.

But why did the conflict kick off in the first place? From the moment Charlie's head was crowned in 1625, right up until it was chopped off in 1649, the king was falling out with Parliament over three main things: religion, power, and money.

Religion was one of the big political topics of the day England had been officially Protestant since 1559, but there was plenty of disagreement over what religious worship should involve. Some, like the Puritans and Presbyterians, thought worship should be very plain and serious and that further reformation of the Church was required. But others who had the support of the King, held different views and preferred elaborate and ceremonial styles of worship, which Puritans suspected as being too close to Catholic ritual. Charles was suspected of being sympathetic of Catholics and even married the Catholic Princess, Henrietta Maria of France, which made him deeply unpopular with the Puritans.

Charles was a big believer in the Divine Right of Kings, the idea that he'd been hand-picked to rule by God himself and that he should rule as he saw fit. The trouble was many parliamentarians liked to have their say on how the country was governed. And while Parliament wasn't as powerful as it is today, with the monarch able to summon or dismiss it at any time, they did have one crucial function, the ability to raise money through tax, which brings us to money.

Charles kicked off his reign with a war against Spain, but the first big expedition ended in failure after the English soldiers got drunk in Cadiz in 1625. An absolutely fuming Parliament refused to grant more taxes for the war, so instead the king levied a forced loan and imprisoned those who refused to pay. Parliament tried to stop him,
#### Parliament Dismissed - 1629
but in 1629, Charles simply dismissed Parliament and didn't recall it for another 11 years, deciding that he'd rather go without taxes than deal with his opponents. Now that's a power play.

Without taxes, Charles couldn't afford to go to war, so he decided to exploit old laws to raise revenues and introduced new money-making schemes, none of which were popular and he was still managing to annoy people with his religious policies. In fact, so annoyed were the Scots at being told to use a new Book of Common Prayer that they waged a rebellion against Charles in 1639.

Charles recalled Parliament in 1640 to raise money for the war, but dismissed it again three weeks later. Then the Scots invaded and occupied most of Northern England, leaving Charles no choice but to bring Parliament back once again to pay them off. Parliament was not in the mood to play nice. They impeached some of the king's key advisors and even executed one of them.

Charles agreed to some of their demands but in January 1642, he marched into Parliament with 400 soldiers and tried to arrest five of his main opponents But the men had escaped. This was not a good look for Charles and fearing for the security of his family if they stayed in London, he left the capital and traveled to York for his safety.

Both sides printed propaganda to rile up their supporters, and across the country, cities and regions decided which corner to back, team Parliament, nicknamed the Roundheads, or team Charles, also known as the Cavaliers. It all came to a head on the first of June 1642, when Parliament published the 19 propositions, which proposed religious reforms and sweeping powers for Parliament within the King's government. Charles rejected it, and after a few weeks of skirmishes and scuffles, he raised his standard at Nottingham on the 22nd of August 1642.

### English Civil War - 1642
Civil War had begun.

So there we have it. After years of tension and debate between reform-minded parliamentarians and the King, mostly over religion, power, and money, England was dragged into the catastrophe and chaos of a full-blown civil war.

### Charles I Executed - 1649
As the war progressed, Charles lost battle after battle and eventually lost his head on January 30, 1649

Historians usually divide the conflict into three separate wars
1. First War (1642–1646): Fought between King Charles I and Parliament, ending in a royalist defeat and the king's surrender.
2. Second War (1648–1649): A brief royalist uprising that led to the trial and execution of King Charles I.
3. Third War (1650–1651): Fought between Scottish supporters of Charles II and Parliament's forces, ending with a decisive parliamentary victory at the Battle of Worcester.

After years of fighting, the Parliamentary forces gained the upper hand, and in 1649, they charged Charles I with treason and beheaded him. The monarchy was dissolved, and England became a republic: a state without a king. Oliver Cromwell headed the new English Commonwealth, and the period known as the English interregnum, or the time between kings, began.

Though Cromwell enjoyed widespread popularity at first, over time he appeared to many in England to be taking on the powers of a military dictator. Dissatisfaction with Cromwell grew. When he died in 1658 and control passed to his son Richard, who lacked the political skills of his father, a majority of the English people feared an alternate hereditary monarchy in the making. They had had enough and asked Charles II to be king. 

### Charles II - 1660
After 11 years of England without a monarchy, Charles II was invited back to be king in 1660. However, he not only had to forgive all those responsible for the Civil War, he also had to agree to restrictions on royal power, made by Parliament and accepted by his dad back in 1641. Charles' acceptance of these terms brought about a new era for the monarchy and no future King or Queen would ever rule with the same absolute power again.

### James II - 1685
King James II (Figure 4.7), the second son of Charles I, ascended the English throne in 1685 on the death of his brother, Charles II. James then worked to model his rule on the reign of the French Catholic King Louis XIV, his cousin. This meant centralizing English political strength around the throne, giving the monarchy absolute power. Also like Louis XIV, James II practiced a form of Roman Catholicism after he converted from Protestantism in the late 1660s. He had a Catholic wife, and when they had a son, the potential for a Catholic heir to the English throne became a threat to English Protestants. James also worked to modernize the English army and navy. The fact that the king kept a standing army in times of peace greatly alarmed the English, who believed that such a force would be used to crush their liberty. As James’s strength grew, his opponents feared their king would turn England into a Catholic monarchy with absolute power over her people.

In 1686, James II applied his concept of a centralized state to the colonies by creating an enormous colony called the Dominion of New England. The Dominion included all the New England colonies (Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Plymouth, Connecticut, New Haven, and Rhode Island) and in 1688 was enlarged by the addition of New York and New Jersey. James placed in charge Sir Edmund Andros, a former colonial governor of New York. Loyal to James II and his family, Andros had little sympathy for New Englanders. His regime caused great uneasiness among New England Puritans when it called into question the many land titles that did not acknowledge the king and imposed fees for their reconfirmation. Andros also committed himself to enforcing the Navigation Acts, a move that threatened to disrupt the region’s trade, which was based largely on smuggling.

### Glorious Revolution - 1688
In England, opponents of James II’s efforts to create a centralized Catholic state were known as Whigs. The Whigs worked to depose James, and in late 1688 they succeeded, an event they celebrated as the Glorious Revolution while James fled to the court of Louis XIV in France. William III (William of Orange) and his wife Mary II ascended the throne in 1689.

### Bill of Rights - 1689
The Glorious Revolution led to the establishment of an English nation that limited the power of the king and provided protections for English subjects. In October 1689, the same year that William and Mary took the throne, the 1689 Bill of Rights established a constitutional monarchy. It stipulated Parliament’s independence from the monarchy and protected certain of Parliament’s rights, such as the right to freedom of speech, the right to regular elections, and the right to petition the king. The 1689 Bill of Rights also guaranteed certain rights to all English subjects, including trial by jury and habeas corpus (the requirement that authorities bring an imprisoned person before a court to demonstrate the cause of the imprisonment).

The role of the British Prime Minister developed gradually during the 18th century, with 1721 widely recognized as the definitive starting point.
