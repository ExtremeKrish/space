## 16th Century - The Age of European Arrival

### The Portuguese in India - 1498 onwards
- 8 July 1497 - Vasco da Gama set sail from Lisbon with a small fleet, rounding the Cape of Good Hope.
- 20 May 1498 - Vasco Da Gama's fleet reached Kappad, India, near Calicut (present-day Kozhikode, Kerala), opening the first direct sea route between Europe and India.
- He met the **Zamorin** (ruler) of Calicut, and despite resistance from Arab traders who already controlled the spice trade there, he obtained limited permission to trade.
- This voyage broke the Arab-Venetian monopoly on the India-Europe spice trade and triggered a rush of European powers (Portuguese, Dutch, French, English) to establish a presence in India.
- 1509 - Afonso de Albuquerque became the second Portuguese Governor of the East.
**Capturing of Goa**
- **March 1510** - Albuquerque, with help from the Vijayanagara Empire, defeated the Bijapur Sultanate and captured **Goa**, making it the permanent capital of the Portuguese possessions in Asia ("Estado da Índia").
- The Portuguese introduced the **Cartaz system**, a naval permit that any non-Portuguese ship in the Indian Ocean had to purchase, effectively taxing all sea trade in the region.
- For nearly a century (1500-1600) the Portuguese held a near-monopoly over European trade in the Indian Ocean, with other bases at Daman, Diu, Bombay (Bassein), Cochin, and Nagapattinam.
- Portuguese control began declining from the early 1600s onward due to competition from the Dutch and English, and Portugal's own annexation by Spain (1580-1640) weakening its resources.

Back in the 16th century, the Dutch privateers were in control of the spice trade.

In 1599, Britain had no direct control over the global pepper trade. Instead, **Dutch merchants** and the **Portuguese** controlled the supply and prices of pepper in Europe.

In September of 1599, they decided to increase the price of pepper by 5 Shillings.

This angered the British merchants. So, on 24th September, 1599, at Leadenhall Street in London, 24 merchants held a meeting and decided to establish a new trading firm.

They roped in 125 shareholders who put together a total currency of 72,000 Pounds, which was used as the initial capital for the company.

#### Battle of Sawlley 
- 1612 - In late 1612, the East India Company sent Captain Thomas Best with armed merchant ships (galleons) to the port of Surat.
- A much larger Portuguese naval fleet attacked the English ships off the coast of Swally (near Surat). Despite being outnumbered, Captain Thomas Best used superior naval tactics and artillery to defeat the Portuguese fleet in a multi-day battle.
- Until then, the Mughals viewed the Portuguese as the undisputed masters of the sea. Witnessing a small English force decisively defeat the powerful Portuguese navy right in front of Mughal observers completely shifted their perspective. The Mughal court realized that the English possessed significant naval strength and could protect their own trade.
- **1613** - Deeply impressed by the English naval victory at Swally and seeking a counterweight to Portuguese influence, Jahangir issued a farman permitting the English to build a permanent factory (a trading post/warehouse, not a manufacturing plant) at **Surat**, this was the Company's first permanent Indian trading post.
- Even though the English had a factory in Surat, local Mughal governors and officials frequently harassed them, imposed heavy taxes, and extorted bribes. The EIC realized that local permissions were not secure enough.
- Sir Thomas Roe landed at Surat on September 18, 1615, as an official ambassador from King James I to the court of Mughal Emperor Jahangir. His mission aimed to secure trading rights and protect English merchants of the East India Company.
- 1615-1618 - **Sir Thomas Roe**, stayed at Jahangir's court and secured farmans allowing the Company to trade and build factories across Mughal territory.
- In year 1620, they opened a third factory in Patna. But it didn't work so they had to close it in a year.
- By the 1620s the Company had factories at Surat, Broach, Ahmedabad, Agra, and Masulipatnam (machilipatnam).

### The Bengal Scenario
In 1651, the British EIC secured a critical privilege from the Mughal Prince **Sultan Shuja** (the Governor of Bengal at the time). He granted them a nishan (royal permit) that allowed the English to trade duty-free in exchange for a single, fixed annual payment of just 3,000 rupees.

This tax loophole made Bengal the EIC's absolute goldmine. They exported massive amounts of Silk and Fine Cotton (highly demanded in Europe) & Saltpeter (the crucial raw ingredient needed to make gunpowder). 

By the late 1670s, Bengal trade accounted for roughly 60% of all British imports from Asia. Their export values skyrocketed from around £24,000 in the 1660s to nearly £150,000.

In 1680, Emperor Aurangzeb issued a brand-new imperial firman (decree) to clean up trade rules across the empire. In this law, he declared that all Christian traders must pay a 3.5% customs tax on their goods.

> [!info] Maratha Empire

Because the original 1651 permit was just a letter from a prince & not a permanent law signed by the Emperor, local Mughal customs officers (faujdars) started ignoring it. As the English grew wealthier, local officials stopped their cargo ships along the Hooghly River, demanding a 3.5% customs tax on every shipment and extracting heavy bribes before letting goods pass (Those local officials were blocking the ships because they were enforcing that specific 1680 imperial firman issued by Emperor Aurangzeb). The EIC was furious but powerless because they had no military presence to defend their ships.

By 1681, the corporate bosses in London decided that Bengal trade was too massive to be handled by minor clerks. They upgraded Bengal into an independent agency and sent William Hedges to fix the tax crisis.

The English East India Company sent Sir William Hedges to Bengal in 1682 (he was appointed in late 1681 and arrived in July 1682). He met with Shaista Khan, who was the Mughal governor (Subahdar) of Bengal and mama of Mugal Emperor Aurangzeb.

### Anglo-Mughal War / Josiah Child's War - 1686-1690
- Emboldened by growing profits, the Company (under Josiah Child in London) attempted an armed confrontation with Emperor **Aurangzeb**, attacking Mughal shipping and ports.
- The Mughals crushed the Company's forces, blockaded its factories of Hougli, Patna, Kasim Bazaar, Masulipatnam & Vishakha Patnam, closed the factory at Surat & captured the trade in Bombay, and forced it into complete surrender. The Company had to pay a heavy fine and beg for a pardon to be allowed to keep trading.
- By late 1689, the EIC was left with virtually nothing but a single besieged, starving fort in Bombay. They had lost every single trade factory and asset they owned on the subcontinent.
- After Aurangzeb’s massive army completely crushed the British fleets and besieged Bombay, the East India Company was forced to surrender. In 1690, Company envoys literally prostrated themselves at Aurangzeb's court to beg for mercy.
- According to some accounts, British officials in Surat were paraded in chains on the streets of Surat, while some went to Aurangzeb with their hands tied behind their backs seeking mercy. Sir John Child offered to pay the Mughal a total of Rs 1.50 lakh as fine. His apologies were accepted, as were those of British officials in Bengal, Surat and Fort St. George. In 1698, the same Aurangzeb allowed the East India Company to construct Fort William in Bengal.[^8]
## 18th Century

### Aurangzeb's Death and the Succession Crisis - 1707
- Aurangzeb, the last of the "Great Mughals," died in 1707, leaving a vastly overextended empire drained by decades of war in the Deccan.
- A rapid succession of weak emperors followed for 10 years straight, and central authority collapsed as powerful regional governors (subahdars) began ruling their provinces as semi-independent states while still nominally acknowledging the Mughal emperor in Delhi.

The only source of revenue left by the Mughal Empire was Bengal.
- Out of this fragmentation rose the regional powers that would dominate 18th-century India: the **Marathas** (in the west and Deccan), the **Nizam of Hyderabad**, the **Nawab of Bengal**, the **Nawab of Awadh**, and the **Nawab of the Carnatic** (in the south), among others.

### Nadir Shah's Invasion - 1739
- The Persian ruler **Nadir Shah** invaded India, crushed the Mughal army, and sacked Delhi, carrying off enormous wealth including the **Peacock Throne** (Made with over 1,150 kg of solid gold and 230 kg of precious stones. Cost roughly ten million rupees, double the expense of building the Taj Mahal) and the **Koh-i-Noor diamond**.
- This invasion is often cited as the moment that exposed just how hollow Mughal military power had become, and it accelerated the scramble by regional and foreign powers to fill the vacuum.

### The Carnatic Wars (Anglo-French Rivalry in India) - 1746-1763
- With the Mughal centre gone, the English and French East India Companies increasingly fought each other by proxy, backing rival claimants to local thrones in exchange for trading privileges — a pattern set during the **Carnatic Wars**.
- **First Carnatic War (1746-1748)** - An extension of the War of Austrian Succession in Europe; the French, under Governor **Dupleix**, captured Madras from the British before it was returned by treaty.
- **Second Carnatic War (1749-1754)** - Fought over rival claimants to the thrones of Hyderabad and the Carnatic; this is where a young **Robert Clive** first rose to prominence, notably defending Arcot in 1751.
- **Third Carnatic War (1758-1763)** - Fought alongside the Seven Years' War in Europe; the decisive **Battle of Wandiwash (1760)** saw the British crush French forces under Lally.
- **Treaty of Paris (1763)** ended the war — the French were allowed to keep their trading posts (like Pondicherry) but were barred from maintaining fortifications or armies, permanently ending them as a serious political/military rival to the British in India.

- He was a very strict ruler, & he didn't liked british activities, he had 3 problems with britishers. - June 20, 1756 - Alarmed by unauthorized British fortification of Calcutta, Nawab Siraj ud-Daulah attacked and captured the British East India Company's Fort William and factory in Calcutta on **June 20, 1756**, leading a massive army estimated between 30,000 and 50,000 troops; the subsequent, historically disputed "**Black Hole of Calcutta**" incident happened (in which a number of British prisoners reportedly died overnight in a cramped cell) was used by the British to justify retaliation.
- The British East India Company dispatched a retaliatory military expedition from Madras in late 1756 led by **Robert Clive** and **Charles Watson** to recapture Calcutta. 
- In early 1757, and the Company then conspired with disaffected Bengali nobles — most importantly Siraj-ud-Daulah's own army commander, **Mir Jafar** — to overthrow the Nawab, with the financial help of Jagat Seth.

### Battle of Plassey - 23 June 1757
- Clive's roughly 3,000 troops faced Siraj-ud-Daulah's much larger army, but Mir Jafar's forces stood down without fighting as pre-arranged, and Siraj-ud-Daulah was defeated and later killed.
- The Company installed Mir Jafar as a puppet Nawab of Bengal in exchange for massive payments and trading concessions.
- Plassey is conventionally treated as the starting point of British political (not just commercial) power in India — the Company now had a de facto foothold as kingmaker in India's richest province.

### Battle of Buxar - 22 October 1764
- Bengal's puppet-Nawab arrangement proved unstable; Mir Jafar's successor **Mir Qasim** tried to assert real independence and allied with the Mughal Emperor **Shah Alam II** and the Nawab of Awadh, **Shuja-ud-Daula**, to expel the British.
- The combined Indian force (~40,000) was decisively defeated at Buxar (in present-day Bihar) by a much smaller British force (~17,000) under **Major Hector Munro**.
- Buxar is often considered more historically decisive than Plassey — it broke the last serious coordinated Indian military attempt to reverse British gains, and it directly forced the Mughal Emperor himself to the negotiating table.

### Treaty of Allahabad - 1765
- Signed between Robert Clive and Emperor Shah Alam II.
- The Mughal Emperor granted the Company the **Diwani** (the right to collect land revenue) of **Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha**, while nominal administrative/judicial authority (Nizamat) stayed with the Nawab.
- This created the unusual "dual government" system — the Company held the purse strings while a puppet Nawab held nominal power — which lasted until 1772, when Governor Warren Hastings had the Company take over direct administration too.
- 1765 is generally treated as the year the East India Company transformed from a trading corporation into a territorial ruling power.

## The Company Becomes a State

### The Regulating Act - 1773
- Passed by the British Parliament to bring some oversight to the Company (which had been operating almost unaccountably), following financial scandals and a **famine in Bengal** (1770) that killed around 70 lakh to 1 Crore people while the Company continued extracting revenue.
- Created the post of **Governor-General of Bengal**, with **Warren Hastings** as the first holder, and established a **Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774)**.
- Hastings was later impeached (1787-1795, eventually acquitted) back in Britain over allegations of corruption and abuse of power in India — a sign of growing British unease over how the Company was governing.

### Pitt's India Act - 1784
- Passed under Prime Minister William Pitt the Younger, this created a **Board of Control** in London with real oversight over the Company's political and administrative decisions, while leaving commercial operations to the Company itself.
- Set up the "dual control" system (Crown oversight + Company administration) that governed British India until 1858.

## Expansion Through War: Mysore and the Marathas

### The Anglo-Mysore Wars - 1767-1799
- Fought in four rounds against the powerful south Indian kingdom of Mysore, first under **Hyder Ali** and then his son **Tipu Sultan** ("the Tiger of Mysore").
- **First (1767-1769)** and **Second (1780-1784)** Anglo-Mysore Wars ended largely inconclusively; the Second ended with the **Treaty of Mangalore (1784)**.
- **Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790-1792)** forced Tipu Sultan to cede roughly half his territory under the Treaty of Seringapatam.
- **Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799)** ended with the British storming Tipu's capital, **Srirangapatna**; Tipu Sultan died defending the city on **4 May 1799**, and Mysore was reduced to a subsidiary state under British control.

### The Anglo-Maratha Wars - 1775-1818
- The Maratha Confederacy, the strongest indigenous power left in India, fought the British across three drawn-out wars.
- **First Anglo-Maratha War (1775-1782)** ended inconclusively via the Treaty of Salbai.

### Subsidiary Alliance System - Lord Wellesley (Governor-General, 1798-1805)
- Wellesley expanded British control largely without direct conquest, through the **Subsidiary Alliance**: Indian rulers were made to disband their own armies, host British troops (paid for by the ruler), and accept a British Resident at court in exchange for "protection" — effectively surrendering their independent foreign policy and military power while nominally staying "independent" rulers.
- Hyderabad, Awadh, Mysore (after 1799), and several Maratha states were brought under this system.

## Reform, Reorganization, and the Road to Formal Crown Oversight

### Charter Acts - 1813 & 1833
- **Charter Act of 1813** ended the Company's monopoly over trade with India (except tea and trade with China), opening India to British private traders and missionaries.
- **Charter Act of 1833** stripped the Company of its remaining commercial functions entirely, turning it into a purely administrative body governing India on behalf of the Crown; the Governor-General of Bengal became the **Governor-General of India** (first: Lord William Bentinck).

**Extreme Centralisation**
- Before 1833, the Presidencies of Bengal, Bombay, and Madras were largely independent and made their own laws. Charter Act of 1833 stripped all independent legislative powers from local provinces and concentrated absolute authority over the entire subcontinent in the hands of a single individual: the **Governor-General of India**.

### Early Social Reform Movements
- **Raja Ram Mohan Roy**, often called the "Father of the Indian Renaissance," campaigned against practices like Sati and championed Western education alongside religious reform; he founded the **Brahmo Samaj** in 1828, a reformist movement within Hinduism.
- **4 December 1829** - Governor-General **Lord William Bentinck**, with Ram Mohan Roy's support, passed the **Bengal Sati Regulation**, formally banning the practice of Sati (widow immolation) in British India.

### English Education Act & Macaulay's Minute - 1835
- **Thomas Babington Macaulay**, as a member of the Governor-General's council, wrote his famous **"Minute on Indian Education" (1835)**, arguing that English should replace Persian/Sanskrit as the medium of higher education and administration in India, to create — in his words — a class of Indians "Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect."
- This laid the groundwork for the English-medium education system that later produced the very Western-educated Indian middle class that would go on to found and lead the Indian National Congress decades later.

### The Anglo-Sikh Wars & Annexation of Punjab - 1845-1849
- After the death of **Maharaja Ranjit Singh** in 1839, the powerful Sikh Empire in Punjab fell into political instability and factional infighting.
- **First Anglo-Sikh War (1845-1846)** ended with the **Treaty of Lahore**, which reduced Sikh territory and imposed British political control, though Punjab remained nominally independent.
- **Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848-1849)** ended in outright British victory; Punjab was formally **annexed** to British India in 1849, bringing virtually the entire subcontinent under direct or indirect Company control.

### The Doctrine of Lapse - Lord Dalhousie (Governor-General, 1848-1856)
- Dalhousie aggressively expanded British territory using the **Doctrine of Lapse**: if a ruler of a princely state died without a *natural* (biological) male heir, the state would be annexed by the Company rather than passing to an adopted heir — overturning centuries of accepted Hindu succession practice.
- States annexed under this doctrine included **Satara (1848)**, **Jaitpur and Sambalpur (1849)**, **Baghat**, **Udaipur**, **Jhansi (1853)** — this is the annexation that later fed directly into Rani Lakshmibai's grievance against the British — and **Nagpur (1854)**.
- **Annexation of Awadh (Oudh) - 1856**: Unlike the Doctrine of Lapse states, Awadh was annexed on the separate grounds of alleged "misgovernance" by its Nawab, **Wajid Ali Shah**, despite Awadh having been a loyal British ally for decades. This annexation is widely regarded by historians as one of the single most important triggers of the resentment that exploded in 1857 — a huge number of Bengal Army sepoys were themselves from Awadh, and many had direct personal or family ties to the deposed royal court.

## On the Eve of 1857
- By the mid-1850s the Company directly or indirectly controlled nearly the entire subcontinent, having gone from a handful of coastal trading posts in 1613 to the dominant political and military power in India in under two and a half centuries.
- Railways and the electric telegraph were introduced into India in the early-to-mid 1850s, tools that — ironically — the British would later use against the rebels once the 1857 revolt broke out.
- **General Service Enlistment Act (1856)** required new sepoy recruits to be willing to serve overseas if ordered — a demand that violated the religious beliefs of many high-caste Hindu sepoys, who considered crossing the sea (*kala pani*) polluting, adding yet another grievance in the Bengal Army on top of Awadh's annexation and the wider unease over British social and religious interference (like the education and Sati reforms above).
