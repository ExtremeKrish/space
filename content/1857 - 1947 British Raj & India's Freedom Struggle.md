### Indian Councils Act of 1861
- Under the Indian Councils Act of 1861, the Council of the Governor-General of India (Viceroy) was expanded to comprise between 13 and 19 members during central legislative sessions. 
- This body included a permanent seven-member Central Executive Council, consisting of the Viceroy, the Commander-in-Chief, and five ordinary members (the fifth of whom was introduced by the 1861 statute specifically to manage financial portfolios). 
- For legislative operations, this core executive was reinforced by the addition of 6 to 12 nominated "Additional Members." so the total strength could become max 19. 
- However, under this act, the Viceroy & Governors were given the power to nominate Indians as "non-official" members in the Additional Members quota, but because the British handpicked everyone directly, they only chose hyper-loyal elites and puppet royal figures who spoke little to no English, rarely attended sessions, and just agreed with whatever the British said. 
- Lord Canning nominated the Raja of Benares, the Maharaja of Patiala, and Sir Dinkar Rao (the Dewan/administrator of Gwalior) to the Central Legislative Council in 1862

**Provincial Executive & Legislative**
After the extreme centralization of 1833 (which stripped provinces of their law-making powers), the 1861 Act initiated the process of legislative decentralization. The legislative powers of the **Bombay** and **Madras** Presidencies were restored.
The Act authorized the creation of new Provincial Legislative Councils for **Bengal** (formed in 1862), the **North-Western Provinces** (formed in 1886), and **Punjab** (formed in 1897).
These councils consisted Provincial Governer, his Provincial Executive Council & the addition of 4 to 8 Additional Members. Every single member was strictly nominated by the provincial Governor. There were no elections.

**1883- Ilbert Bill Controversy**
The Ilbert Bill Controversy of 1883 began when Viceroy Lord Ripon's government introduced a bill allowing qualified Indian judges to try European British citizens in criminal cases. Many British officials and settlers strongly opposed it because they believed Indians were not fit to judge Europeans. Their protests reflected racial discrimination rather than legal concerns. ### Indian Councils Act 1892
The Indian Councils Act of 1892 expanded the number of additional (non-official) members in the **Council of the Governor-General of India** (the Viceroy's Council) to a minimum of 10 and a maximum of 16 legislative members (up from the previous limit of 6 to 12 members).

So now the total strength of Council of the Governor-General of India was upto **24 members**, after including 8 executives members (Governor-General (Viceroy), the Commander-in-Chief, and the 6 ordinary executive members).

**Provincial Legislative & Executive Expansion**
The number of "Additional Members" in the Provincial Legislative Councils was increased; Madras, Bombay, and Bengal provinces expanded to a maximum of **20 additional members** whereas **North-Western Provinces and Oudh** provinces expanded to a maximum of **15 additional members**.

**Start of Indirect Election System**
The 1892 Act introduced a fancy form of **indirect election** system (though they avoided using the word "election" because it sounded too democratic for them). Instead of the Viceroy just picking his loyal royal buddies, local bodies (like universities, municipalities, district boards, and chambers of commerce) could _recommend_ names, and the Governor would appoint them in Provincial Legislative Council.

For the first time, provincial members were granted the right to discuss the local annual budget and ask questions on matters of public interest.

To get into Central Level Legislative Council (Council of the Governor-General of India), the non-official members of the **4 Provincial Legislative Councils** (Bengal, Bombay, Madras, and North-Western Provinces) & the **Calcutta Chamber of Commerce** could recommend the names, and the **Viceroy (Governor-General)** would appoint them to the Central Council i.e. Council of the Governor-General of India.

Because local educated bodies had a say, actual nationalists, intellectuals, and early Congress leaders started entering both Central and Provincial Legislative Councils. 
- Surendranath Banerjee with **Womesh Chandra Bonnerjee** (the first-ever INC President), and **Ananda Charlu** (INC President in 1891) won recommendations to the Bengal and Madras Legislative Councils respectively in 1893 
- **Pherozeshah Mehta** (INC President in 1890) was elected/recommended by the Bombay Presidency non-official council members to the Central Legislative Council in Calcutta in 1893.
- Gopal Krishna Ghokhle got into Central Legislative Council in 1902.

### Morley-Minto Reforms - 1909
Before this Morley-Minto Reforms (1909), the **Council of the Governor-General of India** was tiny, consisting of just **24 total members** (The Viceroy, 6 executive members, and up to 16 legislative members), but under the Morley-Minto Reforms, a new official term was given to this Council, the **Imperial Legislative Council**. It expanded to **70 total members**, which consisted of:
- The Viceroy & his Executive Council (9 members), this was the British council that actually ran the country. The 9 members were powerful administrators who headed major government ministries. They included: The Viceroy, The Commander-in-Chief, Home Member, Finance Member, Revenue and Agriculture Member, Legislative (Law) Member, Public Works Member, Commerce and Industry Member, Education and Health Member.
- 28 Nominated Officials (British bureaucrats who were forced by law to vote _for_ government bills)
- 5 Nominated Non-Officials who were selected by the Viceroy from loyal Indian elites.
- 27 Elected Members, Mostly wealthy Indians elected through highly restricted, indirect voting by local municipal boards and landlords.

Now among these 27 Elected Members, the seats were reserved for various communities & in this Morley Minto reform, **6 seats were reserved for Muslims**, only wealthy Muslim landholders and elites could vote for these 6 seats. 6 seats were reserved for Landholders, 2 were for Chambers of Commerce (Reserved for elite traders, 1 for Calcutta and 1 for Bombay) & 13 seats were for Provincial Legislative Councils (Elected indirectly by the members of local provincial councils).

**Provincial Level**
This reform vastly increased the size of the provincial councils and formally introduced separate communal electorates at the provincial level.
- Major Provinces (Bengal, Bombay, Madras, and the United Provinces) were expanded to a maximum of 50 members, whereas Smaller Provinces (Punjab, Burma, and Assam) were expanded to a maximum of 30 members.
- **Separate electorates were granted to Muslims**, ensuring dedicated seats within the provincial councils also that only Muslim voters could elect. These separate electorates were **heavily pushed by the Shimla Deputation of 1906** (led by Muslim leaders who later formed the Muslim League), which Lord Minto happily agreed to.
- Members could now move resolutions on the budget, suggest amendments, and ask supplementary questions.
- While the Central Council strictly maintained a British Official Majority (so the British could never lose a vote), the Provincial Legislative Councils were allowed to have a Non-Official Majority for the first time. (Though the British still controlled things because "non-officials" included loyal Indian zamindars nominated by the Governor).

### The Lucknow Pact  - 1916
- Dec, 1916 - The Lucknow Pact of 1916 was an agreement between the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League to jointly pressure the British government for more liberal policies in India. It was led by Bal Gangadhar Tilak for the Congress and Muhammad Ali Jinnah for the Muslim League, alongside Congress session president Ambica Charan Mazumdar and leaders like Mahatma Gandhi and Sarojini Naidu.

The main clauses of the Lucknow Pact were:
1. There shall be self-government in India.
2. Muslims should be given one-third representation in the central government.
3. There should be separate electorates for all the communities until a community demanded for joint electorates.
4. System of weightage should be adopted.
5. The number of the members of Central Legislative Council should be increased to 150.
6. At the provincial level, four-fifth of the members of the Legislative Councils should be elected and one-fifth should be nominated.
7. The strength of Provincial legislative should not be less than 125 in the major provinces and from 50 to 75 in the minor provinces.
8. All members, except those nominated, were to be elected directly on the basis of adult franchise.
9. No bill concerning a community should be passed if the bill is opposed by three-fourth of the members of that community in the Legislative Council.
10. Term of the Legislative Council should be five years.
11. Members of Legislative Council should themselves elect their president.
12. Half of the members of Imperial Legislative Council should be Indians.
13. Indian Council must be abolished.
14. The salaries of the Secretary of State for Indian Affairs should be paid by the BritishGovernment and not from Indian funds.
15. Out of two Under Secretaries, one should be Indian.
16. The Executive should be separated from the Judiciary.

### Rowlette Act - March 1919
The Rowlatt Act was passed on March 18, 1919, by the Imperial Legislative Council in Delhi. The law was designed to grant the colonial government sweeping powers to crush nationalist movements, censor the press, and detain political suspects for up to two years without a trial.

This Act had been hurriedly passed through the Imperial Legislative Council despite unanimous, united opposition from every single Indian member. Outraged by the colonial high-handedness, prominent Indian leaders including Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Madan Mohan Malviya, and Mazhar-ul-Haq resigned from the Imperial Legislative Council in protest.

Mahatma Gandhi wanted non-violent civil disobedience against such unjust laws, which would start with a hartal on 6 April. Rallies were organised in various cities, workers went on strike in railway workshops, and shops closed down. Alarmed by the popular upsurge, and scared that lines of communication such as the railways and telegraph would be disrupted, the British administration decided to clamp down on nationalists. Local leaders were picked up from Amritsar, and Mahatma Gandhi was barred from entering Delhi.

### Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms - Dec 1919
**Central Level:-**
The British Parliament passed the _Government of India Act 1919_ (the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms), which ordered the abolition of the old **Imperial Legislative Council** and the creation of the new split bicameral system consisting:
1. **Council of State** (Upper House) - having 60 members, out of those 60
	1. **33 Elected Members**: The voting qualifications for this house were incredibly high. Only the wealthiest landlords, top-tier businessmen, and people with high titles (like _Rai Bahadur_) could vote.
	2. **27 Nominated Members**: Handpicked by the Viceroy, consisting of 20 British officials and 7 non-officials.
2. **Central Legislative Council** (Lower House) - having 145 members max. Out of those 145 members there were 
	1. **104 Elected Members** (The Democratic Face): These seats were filled through direct elections, but only about 3% of the Indian population had the right to vote. Voting rights were restricted to wealthy landowners, taxpayers, and educated elites. Furthermore, the British used a strategy called "Separate Electorates" to divide voters by religion and interest: 
		- **General Seats**: 52 (mostly won by Hindu candidates), 
		- **Muslim Seats:** 30 (only Muslims could vote for these candidates), 
		- **Sikh Seats:** 2 
		- 20 seats were reserved for European merchants, Indian landlords, and chambers of commerce.
	2. **41 Nominated Members** (The British Safety Net): These members did not have to run for election. The British Viceroy handpicked them to ensure the government always had a loyal voting bloc. 
		- **Officials:** 26 were British bureaucrats or colonial officers. 
		- **Non-Officials:** 15 were Indian loyalists, minorities, or specialists chosen by the Viceroy.

The **Central Executive Council** was seprated & formed the top tier. *They were not responsible to the legislature*. This means the Upper and Lower houses could not vote to fire them or dissolve the cabinet. However, to guide government bills, the 8 executive members were given automatic seats inside the houses as part of the **Nominated Officials** quota (usually sitting in the Lower House to defend British policies. Btw they did not hold seats in both houses at the same time, the Viceroy assigned executives to the House where they were needed most).

If the Lower and Upper houses rejected a law proposed by the Executive Council, the Viceroy used his supreme veto power to bypass both houses completely and enact it into law anyway.

**Provincial Level**
While the 1919 Act introduced a bicameral legislature at the _Center_, it introduced a complex system of **Dyarchy (Double Rule)** at the _Provincial_ level. The size of the provincial councils was radically expanded to look more like actual state assemblies. 
- Bengal's council increased to 140 members (114 elected, 26 nominated/official), Madras's council increased to 132 members (98 elected, 34 nominated/official), The United Provinces comprised 111 members (86 elected, 25 nominated/official), Bombay council comprised 111 members (86 elected, 25 nominated/official), and Punjab numbered 94 members (71 elected, 23 nominated/official).
- The Act legally required a specific ratio for these new, larger houses: **At least 70%** of the members had to be **Elected** (through direct elections, though voting rights were still restricted to roughly 3-10% of the population based on tax, property, and education). Separate electorates were expanded to include Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans. **A maximum of 20%** could be **Official Members** (British bureaucrats), & the remaining ~10% were **Nominated Non-Officials** chosen to represent minorities or special interests.
- Provincial administration (and thereby the legislative power) was divided into two strictly separate categories: Transferred Subjects & Reserved Subjects, 
- The first body, Transferred Subjects included subjects like Education, Health, Agriculture, and Local Self-Government. These were administered by the Governor with the aid of **Indian Ministers**. These Indian Ministers were chosen from the elected members of the Provincial Legislative Council and were directly _responsible to the council_ (meaning they could be questioned, heavily criticized, and forced to resign by the legislature). 
- The Reserved Subjects included crucial subjects like Police, Justice, Finance, and Land Revenue. These were kept firmly under British control. They were administered by the Governor and his **Provincial Executive Council** of bureaucrats. ### Simon Commission  - 1928
To investigate the functioning of the 'Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms' of 1919 (Government of India Act 1919), the British government formed the Simon Commission in 1927. It was meant to see what further constitutional reforms or steps toward democracy could be taken in India in the future, and whether India had become capable of governing itself or not. The commission had a total of 7 British MPs, and its head was Sir John Simon. One of its members was Clement Attlee, a Labour politician who would later become Prime Minister of the United Kingdom in 1945. Not a single Indian was included in the commission.

The exclusion of Indians from a commission that was supposed to decide the constitutional future of India caused widespread anger. The commission was strongly opposed by both the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League. Prominent Indian leaders including Jawaharlal Nehru, Mahatma Gandhi, and Muhammad Ali Jinnah also opposed it. Indians argued that when the future of their country and its Constitution was at stake, who were the British to make the decisions alone? Were the British to decide what rights Indians should have? This was seen as a direct affront to India's self-respect, sparking a wave of anger across the nation.

The Simon Commission arrived in Bombay on 3 February 1928. It was immediately met with protests, with people carrying black flags and shouting slogans of "Simon Go Back." Wherever the commission went, it was greeted with similar demonstrations and widespread opposition across India.

Lord Birkenhead, the British Secretary of State, challenged Indian leaders to draft an agreed-upon constitution to prove they could govern themselves. An All-Parties Conference formed a committee chaired by Motilal Nehru to write this founding constitutional blueprint:
**The Nehru Report** (1928): Congress drafted a future constitution for India that in some ways rejected separate electorates for Muslims, deeply alienating Muslim League leaders. [^14]

### Netaji's Struggle
On 16th January 1941, Bose sneaked out of his Calcutta house at night disguised as an insurance agent, Muhammad Ziauddin, with his nephew Shishir Kumar Bose, and travelled by train via Delhi to Peshawar, where a local Forward Bloc leader received him. He crossed into Afghanistan on 26th January 1941, reaching Kabul by early morning of 29th January.

The 15-day journey from Calcutta stayed undetected for 12 days, since his nephews kept eating the food delivered to his room; the British only learned he was missing on 27th January 1941, when he failed to appear in court, and the news soon spread worldwide through Indian papers and Reuters, though several reports on his whereabouts were wrong.

In Kabul, he went to the Soviet Embassy to ask for help but the Soviet embassy refused to help, suspecting him of being a British agent. 

The German embassy proved more useful: on 5th February 1941 minister Hans Pilger agreed to help him via the Italian ambassador, whom Bose met on 22nd February 1941, and by 10th March 1941 he held a new Italian passport under the identity Orlando Mazzotta. The British, having intercepted intelligence that he was heading to Germany via the Middle East, sent agents to kill him in Turkey, but Bose slipped past them via Moscow instead, **reaching Berlin on 2nd April 1941.**

In Berlin, Netaji pursued three goals: an Indian government-in-exile, a way to broadcast to India, and an army of Indian prisoners of war. Hitler, who in _Mein Kampf_ had praised British rule in India and mocked Indian freedom fighters, refused to officially back Indian independence, so Netaji treated the alliance as purely *transactional*. He found real support instead in Adam von Trott of the German Foreign Office, and on 2nd November 1941 formally launched the Free India Centre in Berlin.
Six decisions were taken at the first meeting of the Centre:
1. The name of the movement was to be Azad Hind or Free India.
2. The name of the organisation in Europe - Azad Hind Centre.
3. National anthem - Jana Gana Mana.
4. Emblem of the movement: Tricolour with a springing Tiger.
5. Prescribed greeting among Indians - Jai Hind.
6. A title for Subhas Chandra Bose - Netaji.

On 19th February 1942, he dropped the Mazzotta identity and made his first **Azad Hind Radio** broadcast, followed by a monthly journal named **"Azad Hind"** in March 1942, with 5,000 copies circulating in Germany within days. #### 1944: Rangoon, Imphal, and Retreat
In 1944, his government signed a loan agreement with Japan as a co-equal partner rather than a client, drawing only 10 million of the 100 million yen offered. On 7th January 1944, he moved his headquarters from Singapore to Rangoon, closer to India, setting his sights on Imphal and Kohima.

The Imphal offensive ran from 3rd March to 18th July 1944, pitting around 100,000 INA and Japanese troops against the British Indian Army. Early success, including the tricolour's first hoisting on the Indian mainland, at Moirang, gave way to disaster as an early monsoon in May 1944 turned the ground to mud, Japan's Pacific losses left the INA without air cover, and British attacks cut off supplies, forcing troops to survive on grass and wild flowers.

- On 6th July 1944, Netaji first used the title ***"Father of the Nation"*** for Gandhi in a radio address. 
- On 10th July 1944, the Japanese declared the position indefensible, and the INA retreated into Burma; on 21st August 1944, Netaji publicly acknowledged the campaign's failure and returned to Singapore to begin rebuilding the INA.

### Cabinet Mission Plan - March 1946
March 24, 1946, The Cabinet Mission Plan was a proposal by the British government in 1946 to resolve the deadlock between the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League regarding the transfer of power in India. It consisted of three British cabinet members: Lord Pethick-Lawrence (Secretary of State of India), Sir Stafford Cripps, and A. V. Alexander. The plan, drafted by Stafford Cripps, suggested a three-tier federation for India. This structure included a minimal central government in Delhi to handle defense, foreign affairs, communications, and related finances. The subcontinent would be divided into three groups of provinces: 
- Group A (Hindu-majority provinces), 
- Group B (Muslim-majority provinces in the west), and 
- Group C (Bengal and Assam).

### Noakhali Riots
The Noakhali Riots began on October 10, 1946 (the day of Kojagari Lakshmi Puja) and lasted for roughly a month, with intense, unabated peak violence lasting for about a week before transitioning into prolonged sporadic unrest and localized intimidation through early November 1946.

Official British estimates cited about 200 deaths, while independent and contemporary historical research puts the estimated death toll at roughly 5,000 people. Reports confirm hundreds of Hindu women were subjected to gang rape, sexual assault, and forced marriages.

Mass conversions heavily outnumbered the killings, with modern estimates and historical records noting that thousands to upwards of 22,500 people were forcibly converted to Islam under the threat of execution.

Between 50,000 and 75,000 survivors managed to escape the containment areas and sought immediate shelter in temporary relief camps set up in neighboring regions like Comilla, Chandpur, and Agartala. Another 50,000 Hindus remained marooned inside the affected areas under hostile surveillance.

Mahatma Gandhi left for Noakhali on November 6, 1946, at the age of 77, to help stop the communal violence and restore peace.
He walked barefoot across 47 villages over four months, living simply, praying with survivors, and engaging local Muslim populations to subdue immediate terror, his peace mission to Noakhali achieved a powerful moral and symbolic success by halting large-scale killings and calming local panic through his presence.

### Attlee's Declaration - Feb 1947
20 February 1947. The time had nearly arrived when the British announced the final date for their complete withdrawal from India. British Prime Minister Clement Attlee stated in a speech that the British government would grant full self-rule to British India before 3 June 1948.
> “7. His Majesty's Government desire to hand over their responsibility to authorities established by a Constitution approved by all Parties in India in accordance with the Cabinet Mission's plan, but unfortunately there is at present no clear prospect that such a Constitution and such authorities will emerge. The present state of uncertainty is fraught with danger and cannot be indefinitely prolonged. His Majesty's Government wish to make it clear that it is their definite intention to take the necessary steps to effect the *transference of power into responsible Indian hands by a date not later than June, 1948.*”[^11]

This announcement is known as 'Attlee's Declaration.' However, in the tenth paragraph of his speech, he noted that if, by June 1948, all Indian political parties (INC & ML & Others) failed to form an inclusive Constituent Assembly, the British government would not hand over the powers of the central government in Delhi to any single entity.

> “10. After months of hard work by the Cabinet Mission a great measure of agreement was obtained as to the method by which a Constitution should be worked out. This was embodied in their statements of May last. His Majesty's Government there agreed to recommend to Parliament a Constitution worked out, in accordance with the proposals made therein, by a fully representative Constituent Assembly. *But if it should appear that such a Constitution will not have been worked out by a fully representative Assembly* before the time mentioned in paragraph 7, *His Majesty's Government will have to consider to whom the powers of the Central Government in British India should be handed over*, on the same date, *whether as a whole to some form of Central Government for British India or in some areas to the existing Provincial Governments*, or in such other way as may seem most reasonable and in the best interests of the Indian people.”[^11]

In such a scenario, the British would have to consider to whom power should be transferred. They outlined three options: either transfer full power to a single central government, hand over authority directly to provincial governments in certain regions, or devise an alternative course of action deemed most appropriate at the time.

Effectively, this paragraph gave the Muslim League a 'green signal.' They realized that if they remained adamant and continued to boycott meetings, the British might grant independence to individual provinces rather than keeping the country united, thereby clearing the path for the creation of a separate 'Pakistan.' It was this specific clause that shattered the hope for an undivided India and set the country on the path toward Partition.
