## Solutions
Solutions have 2+ Components, one is 
- Solute (which we take less e.g. Suger) & another is **Can be Multiple**
- Solvent (Which is much in qty, **And is always one**)

### Classification
- 1 Solute + 1 Solvent = Binary Solution
- 2 Solute + 1 Solvent = Ternary Solution
- 3 Solute + 1 Solvent = Quaternary Solution

## Concentration Terms
1. Mole Fraction 
	- **Just remember:** No. Of Moles of A and Mole Fraction of A are two diferent things: $n_{A}$ and $X_{a}$ are different. 
	- $\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i = 1$ (sum of X of all components in a solution is One)
2. Mass % *Grass is Green ahh*
3. Volume % *Grass is Green ahh*
4. Mass by Vol Percent
	Mass of Solute (g) present in 100mL of Solvent
	$= \frac{Mass_{Solute} (g)}{Volume_{Solution} (mL)} \times 100$
	
	E.g. : `10% (W/V) aq. NaOH Soln`
	
	Becomes: 10g NaOH present in 100mL Solution
5. PPM
	- mass of solute (g) present in $10^6$ g solution
	- => $ppm = \frac{Mass_{solute}}{Mass_{solution}}\times 10^6$
6. Molarity (M)
	No of Moles-e-Solute Present in Per Litre of **Entire Solution**
	$M = \Large\frac{n_{solute}}{Volume of Solution (L)}$

	$M = \frac{W}{M} \times \large\frac{1000}{Vol_{Soln} (mL)}$

## Solubility
Maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a solvent at a given temperature
- Like dissolved like in Polar/Non-Polar
	- Water = **polar**  |  Oil / benzene = **non-polar** 
	So
	- Salt (polar) → dissolves in water ✅
	- Naphthalene (non-polar) → dissolves in benzene ✅
	- Salt in benzene or Nepthaline in Water → “nope, not my type” 
### Dynamic Equilibrium (for Solubility)
When adding solid into Water, 2 things happen, 
1) Dissolution = mixing + breaking into tiny particles 
	- Salt crystal → breaks into ions → spreads in water
2) Crystallisation = particles leaving solution and forming solid
	- Some dissolved salt → comes back → forms solid crystal again
	- Rasgulle di Chasni becoming crystal on edges.
When both happen at same rate, its DYNAMIC EQUILIBRIUM

### Effect of Temperature
- If dissolution is **endothermic (heat absorbed)** → solubility ↑ with temp
- If **exothermic (heat released)** → solubility ↓ with temp
#### Case 1: Endothermic
Solution needs heat  
So when you give heat → it dissolves more 🔥
👉 Example: sugar in water (more dissolves when hot)
#### Case 2: Exothermic
Solution releases heat  
So adding heat = annoying it 😤  
👉 it dissolves less

### Henry's Law (for gas above liquid) (Gas in Liquid)
Henry’s law states that the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the pressure of that gas above the liquid, at constant temperature.

- Henry’s law: pressure ∝ solubility
- p = $k_{h}x_{gas}$
- Kₕ ↑ → solubility ↓
- Temp ↑ → solubility ↓
- Works only:
    - low pressure
    - no reaction bw gas & liquid
    - dilute solutions
## Vapour Pressure
- Vapour pressure is the maximum pressure exerted by a vapour equilibrium with the liquid in a closed system.
- Vapour pressure represents the pressure of the saturated vapour.
- Vapour pressure **depends only on temperature and nature of liquid**.
- Vapour pressure doesn't depend on volume of the container or amount of the liquid.

### Roult's Law (for Liquid in Liquid)
There are two liquids A & B, both have their pressure if taken pure is 
- $P^0_A$ → Vapour Pressure of A 
- $P^0_B$ → Vapour Pressure of B

- $X_{A}$ = Mole Fraction of A
- $X_{B}$ = Mole Fraction of B

- $P_{A}$ = Partial Pressure of A in Solution
- $P_{B}$ = Partial Pressure of B in Solution
#### Case - 1 : Both Solute & Solvent Volatile

$$
P_{T} = P_{A}+P_{B}
$$
Where
- $P_{A} = P^0_{A} X_{a}$
- $P_{B} = P^0_{B} X_{B}$

**Final Roult's Law:- (in terms of Mole Fraction)**
$$
P_{Total}= P^0_{A}X_{A}+P^0_{B}X_{B} 
$$

**Roult's Law in terms of Moles**
 $$
 \frac{P_{A}}{P_{B}} = \frac{P^0_{A}}{P^0_{B}} \frac{n_{A}}{n_{B}}
 $$
Also
$$
\begin{align*}
P_T &= P_A + P_B \\
&= P_A^o X_A + P_B^o X_B \\
\text{We Know that: }&\quad X_B = 1 - X_A \\
&= P_A^o X_A + P_B^o (1 - X_A) \\
&= P_A^o X_A + P_B^o - P_B^o X_A \\
P_T &= P_B^o + X_A (P_A^o - P_B^o)
\end{align*}

$$
#### Case - 2 : Non Volatile Solute in Volatile Solvent (Solid in Liquid)
$$
P_{T} = P_{B}^0 X_{B}
$$

## Ideal & Non Ideal Solutions
### Ideal Solution
A solution that **obeys the Raoult's law at any composition** is known as **ideal solution**.
→ The enthalpy of mixing of the pure components to form the solution is zero $\Delta_{mix} H = 0$ 
	no heat is absorbed or evolved when the components are mixed
→ The volume of mixing is also zero $\Delta_{mix} V = 0$
	volume of solution would be equal to the sum of volumes of the two components.
*The intermolecular attractive forces between the A-A and B-B are nearly **equal to those between A-B**, this leads to the formation of ideal solution.*
Ideal solution are rare. A real solution at very high dilution can behave ideally.
**Examples :-** n-hexane, n-heptane, bromoethane, chloroethane, benzene, toulene

### Non Ideal Solution
- does not obey Raoult’s law over the entire range of concentration
- VP either high (Positive Deviation) or low (Negative Deviation)
#### Postive Deviation
→ A-B interactions are weaker then A-A and B-B
→ VP is High
→ Example : Mixing of Ethanol and Acetone or Carbon Disulphide and Acetone

#### Negative Deviation
→ A-B interactions are stronger than A-A and B-B
VP is low
→ Example : Chloroform and Acetone
#### Azeotopes
Azeotropes are **binary liquid mixtures** that have **same composition in liquid and vapour phase** and boil at a **constant temperature**.
**Key Property:**
- Vapour composition = Liquid composition
- Hence, **cannot be separated by fractional distillation**

👉 Azeotrope exists at **ONLY ONE specific composition**

- At that composition → vapour = liquid ✅
- If composition changes → vapour ≠ liquid ❌ (no longer azeotrope)

### 4. Osmotic Pressure $(\pi)$
$$
\pi = CRT
$$
WHERE
C = Molarity
R = Gas Constant
T = Temprature (Kelvin!)
$$
\pi = \frac{n_{2}}{V}RT = \frac{W_{2}}{MM_{2}}RT
$$
$$
MM_{2} = \frac{W_{2}RT}{\pi\,V}
$$
**Osmosis:-** the spontaneous, passive net movement of water (solvent) molecules across a semipermeable membrane from a region of higher water concentration to a region of lower water concentration.
**Hypertonic Solution:** A solution with a higher solute concentration than the cell's cytosol, drawing water out.
**Hypotonic Solution:** A solution with a lower solute concentration than the cell's cytosol, forcing water into the cell.
**Isotonic Solution:** A solution with an equal solute concentration compared to the cell's cytosol, resulting in no net water movement
